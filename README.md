# Designing Recognition Ecosystems for Sectors and Regions

*Governance, standards, quality and funding for recognition at scale.*

Course ID: ICB-L02

## About this course

This course is for people who want to shape how credentials are recognised across a whole sector, a nation or several countries. It supports three groups:

- **Professional bodies and sector associations** that want their members' credentials to be understood and trusted by employers and providers.
- **Policy makers and public agencies** that set rules, fund programmes or run national registers and wallets.
- **Standards and wallet community members** who build the technical and semantic layers that recognition depends on.

The course sits at level L5 "Shape" of the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill ECOS "Ecosystem and recognition partnerships" (VRITE pillar: Value), stage A "Ability". At this level you shape national, sector or cross-border recognition ecosystems, lead consortia and influence policy for mutual recognition. At the Ability stage you practise this on realistic tasks in a controlled setting. You work with cases and a project, not with a live ecosystem.

**Prerequisite:** ICB-A11 Recognition Agreements Across Borders and Sectors.

**Workload:** 16 hours of notional learning in a blended cohort. This file contains the self-paced part. Live and workshop elements are described in facilitator notes.

**Assessment:** You build an ecosystem blueprint and present it to a panel. A person assesses it against the rubric in the section "Assessment and badge". The file also contains a final knowledge check, which supports your preparation.

**Badge:** "Designing Recognition Ecosystems for Sectors and Regions (Ability, L5)", alignment code ICSF-ECOS-L5-A. The badge records that you have shown this capability on the assessed task. It does not prove competency in any particular job.

## Ecosystem analysis

This module teaches outcome 1. By the end you can map the actors in a recognition ecosystem, describe what each one wants, and show where trust is established, where it is assumed and where it is missing.

### What a recognition ecosystem is

A recognition ecosystem is the set of organisations, rules, technologies and habits that let one party accept a credential issued by another. Employers, education providers, associations, regulators, wallet providers, registers and learners all take part. Recognition happens when a verifier decides that a credential is good enough for a purpose, such as hiring, admission, licensing or access to funding.

Three ideas organise the analysis in this module:

- **Actors:** who issues, holds, verifies, regulates, funds, builds and governs.
- **Incentives:** why each actor takes part, and what would make it leave.
- **Trust relationships:** who relies on whom, on what basis, and with what evidence.

Start with the purpose. "Recognition" always means recognition for something. A credential recognised for admission to a master's programme may not be recognised for a licence to practise. Write the purpose first, then list the actors that matter for it.

### Actors and their roles

Typical actors in a sector or regional ecosystem include:

- **Issuers:** education and training providers, professional bodies, employers, awarding organisations.
- **Earners:** learners, workers, members, applicants.
- **Verifiers and users:** employers, admissions offices, licensing authorities, procurement teams.
- **Governors and rule makers:** public agencies, regulators, sector councils, standards bodies.
- **Infrastructure providers:** registers, directories, wallet providers, platform operators, accreditation bodies.
- **Funders:** ministries, regional programmes, employers, member fees.

One organisation can hold several roles. A professional body may issue credentials, accredit providers and lobby government. Record each role separately, because each role carries different incentives and different risks. A body that both accredits providers and sells training to them has a conflict of interest that the governance design must address.

### AI systems as actors

AI systems now take part in recognition in their own right. A screening tool may read credentials and rank applicants. A matching service may map skills in a credential to job requirements. A generative tool may draft learning outcomes or credential descriptions. An automated agent may check credential validity on behalf of a verifier.

Treat each such system as an actor with an accountable organisation behind it. Ask four questions: who operates it, what decision does it influence, what data does it read, and who can challenge its result. If the answers are unclear, the ecosystem has a trust gap, even when the technology works well.

### Reading incentives

Incentives explain adoption and failure. A short test for each actor: what does this actor gain from the ecosystem, what does it risk, and what would it do if nothing changed? Incentives are often mixed. A large employer may welcome portable credentials for hiring and fear that they make staff easier to recruit away.

Pay special attention to the **cost bearer** and the **benefit receiver**. When issuers carry the costs and verifiers receive the benefits, expect slow progress unless the design moves some cost or adds a reward.

### Mapping trust

Trust in an ecosystem has sources. Common ones are legal authority, accreditation, shared standards, reputation, technical proof (such as signatures and verifiable data) and personal relationships. For each important trust relationship, record:

1. **Who trusts whom**, for what purpose.
2. **The basis of trust**, for example a regulator's register or an accreditation scheme.
3. **The evidence** the verifier can check without asking the issuer.
4. **The weak point**, where trust relies on assumption or on a single person.

Draw the result as a map. The graphic below shows the pattern in a simple form.

### Quality across the five VRITE pillars

Use the VRITE pillars as a checklist while you map. **Value:** does each actor gain something? **Rigour:** are the credentials assessed to a defined standard? **Identity:** can earners and issuers be identified reliably? **Trust:** is there evidence a verifier can check? **Experience:** is the process usable for earners and verifiers?

**Worked example: A nursing association maps its ecosystem**

A national association of nurses (a professional body) wants employers in its region to accept its continuing-professional-development credentials. The association's policy officer starts with the purpose: "Employers accept CPD credentials as evidence for annual appraisal and for internal promotion."

She lists the actors. Issuers are 14 training providers and the association itself. Verifiers are hospital HR teams and a regional health authority (a public agency). The regulator keeps the register of nurses. One hospital group uses an automated tool to screen internal promotion applications.

She then records incentives. Providers want recognition because it attracts learners. Hospital HR teams want faster verification, but they fear the cost of changing their systems. The regional health authority wants consistent quality across hospitals. The association wants a role in setting the rules.

For trust, she finds that HR teams currently phone providers to confirm certificates, which takes days (the figure in her notes is illustrative: about three working days). The weak point is that no shared register exists, so trust depends on personal contacts. She also finds an AI actor: the screening tool reads certificates as scanned images, and nobody has agreed who is accountable when it misreads one.

Her output is a one-page map with seven actors, five trust relationships and three weak points: no shared register, no accountable owner for the screening tool, and a conflict of interest because the association both issues credentials and accredits other providers.

**Practice activity: Map your ecosystem**

Choose a sector or region for your blueprint. Produce a one-page analysis with:

1. A one-sentence purpose: recognition of what, for what decision.
2. A list of at least eight actors, each with its role or roles.
3. A table with columns Actor | Gain | Risk | What it does if nothing changes.
4. At least five trust relationships, each with basis, evidence and weak point.
5. At least one AI system treated as an actor, with the accountable organisation named.
6. A sketch of the map in the style of the graphic above. Hand-drawn is fine.

Check your work: have you labelled who bears the cost and who receives the benefit? Have you found at least one conflict of interest?

## Governance design

This module teaches the governance part of outcome 2. By the end you can design the decision rights, roles and quality arrangements for a recognition ecosystem in a sector or region.

### Why governance matters

Governance answers who decides, who is accountable and what happens when something goes wrong. Without it, an ecosystem depends on goodwill, and goodwill fades when staff change or money tightens. With it, parties who do not know each other can still rely on shared rules.

Good governance is proportionate. A small sector network needs a light structure. A national ecosystem that touches law needs a formal one. Design for the level of risk and the number of parties.

### Governance layers

Think of governance in layers, each with its own question:

1. **Policy and legal layer:** what laws, public mandates and mutual recognition agreements apply? Typically owned by ministries, regulators and international bodies.
2. **Ecosystem layer:** who sets the shared rules, admits members and settles disputes? Often a consortium, council or steering board.
3. **Quality layer:** how are issuers, credentials and verifiers held to standards? Accreditation, audit, review, complaints.
4. **Operational layer:** who runs registers, directories, trust lists and help desks day to day?
5. **Technical layer:** who maintains the standards profile, test suites and security rules?

Each layer needs a named owner, a decision rule and a way to change the rules. The graphic shows the layers.

### Decision rights and conflicts of interest

Write a short decision-rights table. For each type of decision, name who proposes, who decides, who is consulted and who can appeal. Typical decisions include admitting a new issuer, suspending an issuer, changing the standards profile, setting fees and publishing a trust list.

Manage conflicts of interest openly. Methods include separating accreditation from commercial activity, rotating panel members, publishing declarations of interest and giving earners a route of complaint to a body outside the issuer.

### Quality arrangements

Quality arrangements make trust checkable. Elements to consider:

- **Entry criteria** for issuers and verifiers, such as legal status, assessment practice and data protection.
- **Credential criteria**, such as defined learning outcomes, assessment method and workload where relevant. The 2022 Council Recommendation on micro-credentials lists elements that a micro-credential should describe, and it is a useful reference point in Europe.
- **Monitoring:** periodic review, sampling, audits, complaints handling.
- **Consequences:** warnings, conditions, suspension, removal, with a fair procedure.
- **Transparency:** a public list of accepted issuers and a changes log.

Apply the five VRITE pillars as a design check. Value: is the quality burden proportionate to the benefit? Rigour: are the criteria specific enough to audit? Identity: are issuers and verifiers identified in a way that cannot be faked easily? Trust: can anyone check status without asking the issuer? Experience: can a small provider comply without a legal department?

### Governing AI actors

Add rules for AI actors to the governance design. Reasonable provisions include: AI systems that influence recognition decisions must have a named accountable organisation; earners can ask for human review of decisions that affect them significantly; operators keep records of how the system was configured; and the ecosystem body can ask for evidence of testing. Check any binding rules against applicable law and regulation in your jurisdiction. <!-- VERIFY: applicable national and EU rules on automated decision-making and AI systems used in education and employment settings, before publishing region-specific guidance -->

**Worked example: A regional agency designs governance for a skills ecosystem**

A regional economic development agency (a public agency) wants employers, colleges and training firms to recognise each other's short courses in the logistics sector. The agency's programme manager convenes a consortium of two colleges, five private providers, a logistics employers' association and the regional employment office.

She proposes a three-part structure. A **steering board** of seven people (illustrative number) sets rules and admits issuers. A **quality panel** of three independent reviewers checks applications and handles complaints. An **operator**, hired by tender, runs the public list of accepted issuers.

She writes a decision-rights table. New issuers: the quality panel recommends, the steering board decides, applicants can appeal to an independent chair. Changes to the standards profile: a technical working group proposes, the steering board decides after a 30 day consultation (illustrative period).

She spots a conflict of interest: one college holds two board seats and also supplies most of the training. The solution is to limit each organisation to one vote and to require declarations of interest at every meeting. She also adds a rule for the employment office's automated matching service: the office is the accountable operator, and jobseekers can request human review.

**Practice activity: Draft your governance design**

Using your ecosystem analysis, draft a governance design of about two pages with:

1. A layer diagram or table naming an owner for each of the five layers.
2. A decision-rights table with at least five decisions, showing proposes, decides, consulted and appeal.
3. Entry criteria for issuers (at least four) and a list of monitoring methods.
4. Two named conflicts of interest from your analysis and how you manage each.
5. Three rules for AI actors.
6. A VRITE check: one sentence per pillar on how the design supports it.

## Standards choices

This module teaches the standards part of outcome 2. By the end you can choose and combine standards, data models and policy references for an ecosystem, and justify the choices.

### Standards serve the ecosystem

A standard has value when it helps the ecosystem reach its purpose. Choosing standards is a design act, not a technical afterthought. Poor choices lock actors out, raise costs or leave meaning unclear. Good choices make credentials understandable, verifiable and portable across the actors you mapped in module 1.

Think in four standards areas:

- **Semantics:** what the credential says. Data models, vocabularies, skills and qualification frameworks.
- **Format and proof:** how the credential is packaged and how its authenticity is shown.
- **Exchange:** how credentials move between issuers, wallets and verifiers.
- **Trust and registry:** how issuers and verifiers are identified and listed.

Select for each area and write down the reason. Where a public mandate exists, it usually constrains the choice.

### Status of the reference points

Mark the maturity of each reference point honestly. Three are relevant to this course.

**European Learning Model (ELM) and European Digital Credentials for Learning** [Established]. Institutions across Europe use them. ELM served as the data model for education credentials in the DC4EU wallet pilot. They are a mature option for describing learning achievements and qualifications in Europe.

**EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (2022)** [Established]. This is a stable policy reference that describes elements and principles for micro-credentials. Watch for Commission implementation reporting and for national transpositions, since details differ between Member States.

**EUDI Wallet under eIDAS 2.0** [Emerging]. Member States must offer at least one wallet by 24 December 2026. Germany plans its state wallet ('d-you') for 2 January 2027 with basic functions first. Regulated private sectors must accept the wallet about one year after issuance starts. Readiness varies widely between Member States. Plan with scenarios, not with assumptions about a single date or feature set. Status as of November 2026; check before publishing.

### How to choose

Use five tests for each candidate standard:

1. **Fit:** does it carry the information the purpose needs, such as learning outcomes, level, issuer and assessment?
2. **Reach:** do the actors in your map already use it, or can they adopt it at reasonable cost?
3. **Openness:** is the specification public, and who governs its change?
4. **Maturity:** is it established, emerging or experimental, and what is the fallback if it slips?
5. **Mandate:** does law or public policy require or favour it?

Score the options and write the reasoning. Avoid choices that bind the ecosystem to one supplier. A profile that names standards and requirements, not products, keeps the ecosystem open.

### Profiles and mappings

Most ecosystems need a **profile**: a short document that says which standards are used, which options are allowed and which fields are required. A profile makes interoperability testable. It also gives a place to record mappings between your sector's own frameworks and wider ones, for example a sector competence framework mapped to national qualification levels.

Plan for change. Include a version number, a change process (from your governance design) and a sunset rule for old versions. Keep the profile small. Every extra required field is a cost for every issuer.

### Standards and AI actors

AI systems read and write credential data. Structured, well-described data helps them work accurately and lets people check their results. Ask whether your profile gives machine-readable fields for outcomes, level and evidence, and whether it states how generated or machine-assisted content in a credential must be marked. Decisions on such rules belong to the governance layer, and the profile records them.

## Funding

This module teaches outcome 3. By the end you can estimate the costs of an ecosystem, identify who benefits and who can pay, and plan a model that lasts beyond the start-up grant.

### Why funding fails

Many recognition ecosystems start with a project grant and fade when it ends. The usual reasons are that no one owns the running costs, that the actors who gain most do not pay, and that the plan counted build costs but not upkeep. A funding plan has to cover the whole life of the ecosystem.

### Cost categories

List costs in four groups:

- **Set-up:** analysis, design, consortium building, legal work, standards profile, first technical build, pilot.
- **Running:** operator, registers and trust lists, help desk, hosting, security, quality panel, audits, secretariat.
- **Change:** updates to the profile, support for new standards or regulation, migrations.
- **Participation:** costs borne by issuers, verifiers and earners, such as system changes, staff training and time. These are often hidden and large.

Estimate each group in ranges. Label figures as illustrative when you have no real data. A blueprint that shows ranges and assumptions is more credible than one with a single precise figure.

### Who benefits, who pays

Return to your module 1 analysis. List the benefit each actor receives and its form: time saved, risk reduced, revenue gained, compliance met, reach extended. Then ask which actors can and will pay, in money or in kind.

Common funding sources:

- **Public funding:** ministry or regional programmes, often suited to set-up and to public-good elements such as a trust list.
- **Membership fees:** typical for associations and consortia, tiered by size.
- **Issuer fees:** per credential, per year or for accreditation. Keep them proportionate so small providers are not shut out.
- **Verifier fees:** possible where verification brings clear value, though fees can reduce use. Many ecosystems keep basic verification free.
- **In-kind contributions:** staff time, hosting, expertise.
- **Value-added services:** optional services such as analytics or training, provided they do not compromise neutrality.

### Sustainability

Sustainability means that the ecosystem can keep running at the agreed quality for the period you plan, say five years. Check four things:

1. **Cost coverage:** does the mix of sources cover running and change costs, not only set-up?
2. **Dependency:** does any single funder cover more than a share you can tolerate? Many plans set a limit and name a fallback if a funder leaves.
3. **Neutrality:** does the funding model create pressure on the quality panel or the operator? Separate funding from decisions on admissions and complaints.
4. **Exit and continuity:** what happens to the register and the trust list if the operator or the consortium ends? Plan for transfer.

### Funding and the VRITE pillars

Value shows in the benefit-to-payer match. Rigour shows in quality functions that are funded, not left as volunteer work. Identity and Trust depend on registers and trust lists that need steady operation. Experience suffers if fees or compliance costs push out small providers. The funding plan should state which of these it protects first when money is short.

### Funding and AI actors

AI systems add costs and dependencies: licences for tools, monitoring, evaluation, human review capacity and incident handling. If an AI service decides or recommends within the ecosystem, the funding plan should cover human review capacity and periodic testing. Where an AI operator is a separate organisation, agree who pays for fixes when the system misreads credentials.

## Adoption

This module teaches outcome 4. By the end you can plan how organisations of different types join and use the ecosystem, in what order, with what support and how you will know it is working.

### Adoption is a plan, not a hope

A well-designed ecosystem still fails if organisations do not join or do not use it. Adoption needs sequencing, support and evidence. Treat it as a project with owners, milestones and checks.

### Segment the organisations

Not all organisations adopt for the same reasons. Segment your actors from module 1:

- **Early movers:** motivated, capable, often already using digital credentials.
- **Followers:** will join when peers or customers do, or when a rule requires it.
- **Constrained:** small or under-resourced, need low-cost routes and help.
- **Reluctant:** see risk or little gain, may need rules, incentives or evidence.

Plan different offers for each. A single message for all will reach only the early movers.

### Barriers and enablers

For each segment list barriers and enablers. Barriers are often practical: no budget, no staff time, legacy systems, unclear legal duties, fear of losing control. Enablers include simple onboarding, templates, shared services, training, peer examples and clear benefits.

Regulation can act as an enabler or a deadline. Under eIDAS 2.0, regulated private sectors must accept the EUDI Wallet about one year after issuance starts, which may create demand in some sectors. Readiness varies widely between Member States, so adoption plans for cross-border ecosystems need national variants. Status as of November 2026; check before publishing. <!-- VERIFY: which sectors are regulated for wallet acceptance, and the exact timing of acceptance duties in each Member State of your ecosystem -->

### Sequencing

A typical sequence has four phases:

1. **Pilot:** a small group of early movers tests the profile, governance and tools. Collect evidence and fix problems.
2. **Launch:** open the ecosystem to a first wave, with onboarding support and a public list of participants.
3. **Scale:** extend to followers and constrained organisations with shared services, lower entry costs and sector or regional champions.
4. **Embed:** align with regulation, procurement rules and sector standards so that using the ecosystem becomes routine.

Set entry and exit criteria for each phase, for example the number of issuers live and the share of verifiers who accept credentials in practice. Illustrative numbers help, but real targets need your own data.

### Support and communication

Provide support that matches the barriers. Examples: an onboarding checklist, templates for credential descriptions, a help desk, a test environment, training for HR and admissions staff and short guidance for earners. Communicate benefits per audience: time saved for verifiers, reach for providers, portability for earners.

Do not forget earners. If earners cannot use, share or understand their credentials, verifiers will not see them. Include clear guidance and a route for questions.

### Measuring adoption

Choose a small set of indicators that show use, not only sign-up:

- Number of active issuers and credentials issued.
- Number of verifiers who check or accept credentials, and how often.
- Time from request to verification result.
- Complaints and their resolution time.
- Earner experience, for example a short survey.
- Spread across segments and regions.

Review indicators at set intervals and let the steering board act on them. Report openly, including problems.

### Adoption and AI actors

AI systems can speed or block adoption. A screening tool that cannot read your credential format will lower acceptance. A matching service that explains its results can raise it. Include AI operators in the pilot, test their handling of your credentials, and publish guidance for operators on how to read and use your data correctly. Keep a route for earners to raise problems caused by automated tools.

### Adoption and VRITE

Value drives the case for each segment. Rigour and Trust keep early reputation intact: a poor early issuer can damage the whole ecosystem, so pilot selection matters. Identity supports secure onboarding. Experience determines whether earners and verifiers keep using the ecosystem after the first try. The graphic shows the adoption sequence.

## Assessment and badge

### The assessed task: Ecosystem blueprint presented to a panel

You prepare an ecosystem blueprint for a sector or a region and present it to a panel. A person assesses the blueprint and your answers using the rubric below. Nothing in this file grades the task.

**Task brief.** Choose a sector or region, real or realistic. Produce a blueprint of about 10 to 15 pages (or equivalent) with these parts, built from your module practice outputs:

1. **Purpose and ecosystem analysis:** purpose, actors, incentives, trust map, weak points, AI actors (module 1).
2. **Governance and quality design:** layers, decision rights, entry criteria, monitoring, conflicts of interest, rules for AI actors (module 2).
3. **Standards profile:** choices per area, maturity and fallbacks, required fields, change rule (module 3).
4. **Funding and sustainability plan:** costs, funding mix, dependency and neutrality rules, continuity (module 4).
5. **Adoption roadmap:** segments, phases, support, indicators, risks (module 5).
6. **VRITE statement:** one paragraph per pillar showing how the blueprint supports it.
7. **Sources and assumptions:** what you checked, what is illustrative, what needs verification.

Then present the blueprint to a panel in 15 minutes and answer 15 minutes of questions. Present your reasoning, not only your results.

### Rubric

Each criterion is rated on four levels: 1 Not yet, 2 Developing, 3 Secure, 4 Strong. The assessor rates each criterion and records evidence in the panel record.

| Criterion (outcome) | 1 Not yet | 2 Developing | 3 Secure | 4 Strong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Actors, incentives and trust (outcome 1) | Few actors, no incentives or trust basis | Actors listed, incentives or trust partly described | Actors, incentives and trust relationships described with weak points and AI actors | Insightful analysis that explains cost and benefit gaps and conflicts of interest and drives later design choices |
| B. Governance and quality (outcome 2) | Roles unclear, no quality arrangements | Some roles and criteria, gaps in decision rights or conflicts | Clear layers, decision rights, quality criteria, monitoring and AI rules | Proportionate, auditable design with well-handled conflicts and clear route for appeal and change |
| C. Standards choices (outcome 2) | Choices unexplained or tied to products | Standards named with weak reasons or no maturity view | Choices justified against tests, maturity marked, fallbacks given | Coherent profile with mappings, change rules and honest handling of Emerging items |
| D. Funding and sustainability (outcome 3) | No credible costs or sources | Costs or sources partial, running costs weak | Costs in ranges, benefit and payer match, dependency and continuity addressed | Robust plan with neutrality safeguards, participation costs and tested risks |
| E. Adoption plan (outcome 4) | Generic, no sequence or indicators | Phases without segments, support or measures | Segmented, sequenced plan with support, indicators and risks | Realistic plan with entry and exit criteria, regulatory timing and earner support |
| F. Coherence and VRITE (outcomes 1 to 4) | Parts do not connect | Some links between parts | Parts consistent, VRITE statement meaningful | Parts reinforce each other, trade-offs explained, VRITE used as a design check |
| G. Presentation and panel dialogue (all outcomes) | Unclear, cannot respond to questions | Understandable, partial answers | Clear, answers questions with reasons | Persuasive, handles challenge, admits limits and adjusts |
| H. Verification and honesty about sources (all outcomes) | Unverified claims presented as fact | Some claims checked | Time-sensitive facts marked and illustrative figures labelled | Careful treatment of status, dates and assumptions throughout |

### Pass rule

To pass you need:

- A rating of at least 3 (Secure) on criteria A, B, C, D and E, which carry the four learning outcomes.
- A rating of at least 2 (Developing) on criteria F, G and H.
- A complete panel record signed by at least two panel members.

If you do not pass, the panel gives written feedback and you may revise and re-present once within the cohort window, subject to your programme's rules.

### Evidence submitted for the badge

- The ecosystem blueprint (final version).
- The panel record: ratings per criterion, comments and the panel members' confirmation.

The badge "Designing Recognition Ecosystems for Sectors and Regions (Ability, L5)" with alignment code ICSF-ECOS-L5-A records that you have shown the capability described in this course on the assessed task. It does not prove competency in any particular job.
